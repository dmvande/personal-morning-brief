Title: "I go unto the Father," or, Power for Praying and Working (John 14:12-13)

The Sermon on the Mount taught new disciples to pray as trusting children. Jesus' farewell words address disciples at the end of their training: friends who know his plans and are about to carry on his work. Prayer is to be the channel of power for that work.

**Greater works, for two reasons.** Jesus was going to the Father to receive all power, and his disciples could now ask anything in his name. His death broke sin's power, his resurrection brought eternal life into human nature, and his ascension let him pour out the Spirit. In three years Jesus gathered only about five hundred followers, while Peter and Paul did greater things through his power from the throne.

**Whoever would work must pray.** Much Christian work shows little of Christ's power because believing prayer in his name is missing. To every complaint about weakness, difficulty or lack of success, Jesus gives one answer: believe, and ask in my name. Without this, work may restrain sin but lacks real power. Effective work requires effective prayer first.

**Whoever would pray must work.** Six times in these chapters Jesus gives unlimited promises: "whatsoever," "anything," "what ye will." Many have pleaded them for personal wants and been disappointed, because they tore the promise from its setting. The promises are given to those who live wholly for Christ's work and kingdom. Jesus will not be made the servant of our comfort.

**Each strengthens the other.** Prayer equips us for work, and work teaches us to pray. "To him that hath shall be given." Moses, burdened with a rebellious people, found both the need and the boldness to ask God for great things. As you give yourself to God's work, you will find that nothing less than these great promises will do.

Give yourself to do Christ's works and you will learn to pray. Give yourself to prayer and you will do his works. With disciples bold in prayer, Christ can conquer the world.
