Title: "Father, not what I will," or, Christ the Sacrifice (Mark 14:36)

Within hours, Jesus moves from the calm "Father, I will" of the high priest to the anguished "Not what I will" of the sacrifice in Gethsemane. The second is the source and power of the first.

**Does Gethsemane undercut bold prayer?** If even the Son's plea was refused, can "whatsoever ye will" be meant literally? In fact Gethsemane is the ground of our assurance.

**Two different prayers.** In John 17 Jesus asked for what he knew was the Father's will. In the garden, facing the horror of becoming a curse, his true humanity asked to be spared if possible. "Not what I will" kept that desire from being sin. The cup was not removed, but the prayer of submission was answered in his victory over death.

**Surrender won our assurance.** Christ's obedience reached its height in giving up his will. Because of it he was exalted with power to ask what he will. As always in redemption, he received the opposite of what he won for us: bound that we might go free, cursed that we might be blessed, his prayer unanswered so that ours might be answered.

**Abiding means sharing his surrender.** The same Spirit by which he offered himself works in us. He teaches us to yield our will completely, even in things not sinful, so that it dies with Christ and rises renewed. A will that has become nothing so that God's will may be everything is filled with divine strength to claim what God has promised.

Gethsemane's "not what I will" opens the way to "ask what ye will."
