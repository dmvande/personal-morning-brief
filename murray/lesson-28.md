Title: "Father, not what I will," or, Christ the Sacrifice (Mark 14:36)

Within hours, Jesus moves from the calm "Father, I will" of the high priest to the anguished "Not what I will" of the sacrifice in Gethsemane. The second came later in time, but it is the source and power of the first.

**Does Gethsemane undercut bold prayer?** If even the Son's plea was refused, can "whatsoever ye will" be meant literally? A deeper look shows that Gethsemane is the very ground of our assurance.

**Two different prayers.** In John 17 Jesus asked for what he knew was the Father's will. In the garden, as the powers of darkness came on him and he tasted God's wrath against sin, his true humanity asked to be spared the cup if possible. That desire was not sin. "Not what I will," repeated three times, kept it from sin and is the heart of his sacrifice. The cup was not removed, but the prayer of submission was gloriously answered in his victory over fear and death.

**Surrender won our assurance.** Christ's obedience reached its height in giving up his will, and from it his death on Calvary draws its value. He "learned obedience," and because he humbled himself unto death, God exalted him with power to ask what he will. As always in redemption, he received the opposite of what he won for us: bound that we might go free, made sin that we might be righteous, cursed that we might be blessed. He bore an unanswered prayer so that ours might be answered.

**Abiding means sharing his surrender.** The same eternal Spirit by which he offered himself dwells in us. He teaches us to yield our will completely, even in things not directly sinful, and to wait teachably for the Father's voice. Our will dies with Christ and rises renewed, with holy joy in God's will and holy boldness to claim it.

A will that has become nothing so that God's will may be everything is filled with divine strength. Gethsemane's "not what I will" opens the way to "ask what ye will."
