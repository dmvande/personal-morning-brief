Title: "What wilt thou?" or, Prayer Must Be Definite (Mark 10:51)

Blind Bartimaeus had cried out for mercy, and Jesus knew exactly what he wanted. Yet he asked, "What wilt thou that I should do unto thee?" The man was healed only after he named his need.

**Vague prayer misses the mark.** Some cry for mercy without asking what mercy should do. Others ask to be freed from sin without naming one sin. Still others pray for revival with no particular place where they expect to see it. Definite prayer makes us find our real needs, tests whether our desires are honest and scriptural, and helps us wait for and recognize the answer. Murray notes how marksmen who aimed each bullet defeated troops who fired into the mass.

**Start with your own circle.** Each believer has limited strength and a particular field: family, friends and neighbours. Naming some of them before God brings us into the training-school of faith. Answers in specific matters make our broader prayers believing and effective.

**Family letters and business letters.** Someone abroad on his father's business writes both. Affectionate letters need no reply line by line, but every order expects an answer. Prayer includes loving fellowship, but it also needs this "business" element: clear requests for which we expect a reply. This is part of what Jesus meant in warning against vain repetitions.

**Wishing is not willing.** Jesus asks what we will, not what we wish. A sluggard wishes to be rich but does not will it. Many prayers are passing wishes, soon forgotten, or repeated for years out of duty while we stay content with no answer.

**A surrendered will is a strong will.** Isn't it humbler to state our wishes and leave the rest to God? That is the prayer of submission, for cases where we cannot know his will. The prayer of faith finds God's will in a promise and pleads for it until it comes. "To believe truly is to will firmly." A child who has yielded his will to the Father is trusted to will boldly. Claiming "no will" is often sloth disguised as humility.
